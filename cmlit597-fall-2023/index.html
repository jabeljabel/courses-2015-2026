Course description

This course examines the role of comparative cultural critique and literary scholarship around our digital media ecologies. Against the increasing intensities of the supposedly higher resolution, putatively frictionless dissemination, and the purported instantaneity of digital cultures over the last four decades, this course examines the recent nostalgia for life off-the-grid, the return to the warmth of analogue, and the rejection of screen cultures across the globe.

How did midi and mp3 cultures of compression transform the record industry? What did pixelization do to visuality through digital photography? What does gamification of lifestyles have to do with videogame cultures? Why do Tik-Tok and Instagram continue to be contentious? And ultimately what is the place for the scholar of global cultures in the current environment? This course addresses these and other questions around digital and cyber cultures through focus on distinctions between low and high art, trash culture and ephemera, analogue vs. digitization, mediation vs. remediation, face to face contact and social bubbles, Nielsen TV Insights and engagement rate algorithms, computational analytics and surveillance, normalization and radicalization, media ignorance and media literacy, the glocalization and localization of global platforms, and media studies and post media theory.

The, as yet, not fully tapped new affordances for scholarship make such study not only trendy, but also risky. From early hypertext and electronic literature through AI literary deep fakes, this course prepares students for the gamut of methodological concerns in studying such culture; for instance: Do digital cultures require digital methods? When participatory cultures seemingly render the historical forms of culture authority and capital mute, where and how do other structures arise to supersede such social functions? Is there a continuing value to making a distinction between cultural producers and recipients? How does inequity of access reveal itself and how is it occluded in platformativity? How does a scholar understand both how media corporations handle data and how not to repeat such handling in their scholarship? Against the putative death of long form prose literature, recent years have witnessed the return of poetry and microfiction distributed on non-print formats. What is the continued efficacy of concepts of “literature” and “the literary” in the context of short attention span, dopaminergic media ecologies?

The course syllabus will vary depending on the interests of the seminar participants, but will likely include readings by many of the following scholars: Marshall McLuhan, Niklas Luhmann, Friedrich Kittler, Yoneji Masuda, Jay David Bolter, Richard Grusin, Wendy Hui Kyong Chun, Jodi Dean, Lisa Gitelman, Katherine Hayles, Henry Jenkins, George Landow, Jerome McGann, and Jussi Parikka.
