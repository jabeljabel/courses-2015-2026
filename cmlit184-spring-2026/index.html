Course content description

This course is designed to introduce students to the art of the short story. During the semester we will read short stories from various cultures and countries, ranging from stories written in the hundreds of years ago to those written within the last few years. This course is intended to help students learn how to read global fiction, how to understand it in its contexts, and how to talk about it.

The desire to tell stories and to be told stories is one of the most basic human needs. All cultures have been defined in part by the stories circulating within them. We are not born knowing how to read the short story or any fiction for that matter. Rather it is a skill that one acquires, and the more one does it, like playing a sport or any other activity, the better we become at it. We will explore the historical development of the short story genre, and examine how historical contexts relate to the content and style of the stories under discussion. We will become familiar with how stories are put together, the difference between simply stories that are short and the modern genre of the short story.

CMLIT/ENGL 184 is not required for the CmLit major but may be selected to fulfill one of the course requirements for the major or the World Literature Minor. This course also fulfills the General Education Humanities requirement, the Bachelor of Arts Humanities requirement, and International Cultures requirement. Though the material originates from around the world, all readings will be provided in English. Students who are able to read in the original languages are encouraged to do so.

This semester we will explore short stories in World Literatures with a thematic focus on short short fiction. Although short short fiction (also known as flash fiction or microfiction) is typically opposed to the long-form novel, this class will challenge this convention by discussing how today’s attention economy increasingly compels writers to condense and parcel their narratives into forms that resemble aphorisms, jokes, and witticisms—graffiti, sound bites, and tweets. Does this cultural form encourage reductive, essentialist thinking and negative behavior or does it provide belonging, insight, and consensus.

Students will a) examine the sociohistorical and cultural contexts that inform the production, circulation, and reception of short stories around the world; b) study the literary techniques, narrative devices, and styles that contribute to the popularity of the short story genre; c) critically analyze or creatively employ these techniques, narrative styles, and styles in their own interpretive and creative writing.

Authors may include

Area of study

This is a course in Comparative Literature, a field that studies literature from an interdisciplinary and global perspective. Comparative literature crosses the boundaries of geography, time, nationalities, languages, and cultures, paying attention to the complicated relationships within and between different groups of people through cultural products. Class discussion may include attention to forms of inequality and social situation including but not limited to political beliefs, economic conditions, historical events, religious differences, gender constructions, sexual behaviors, social status, geographic origin, and racialized identities as these arise in the materials covered during the semester.

Expectations: learning outcomes, objectives, and skills

  1. Read and discuss, orally and in writing, the aesthetic, social, cultural, historical, psychological, aspects of works of literature.
  2. Be able to analyze literature—the short story genre, in this case––in terms of theme, character development, point of view, symbols, images, plot, values, setting, structure and form, and effectively communicate observations, syntheses, and analyses in class discussions.
  3. Be able to evaluate, revise, and adapt your own preconceptions, impressions, and opinions.
  4. On examinations students should be able to answer short identification and essay questions concerning the literary works studied in class.
  5. Acquire the basics for further study in Comparative Literature, English, and world cultures.
  6. Understand narrative strategies, tropes, contents and forms of the genre of short story.
  7. Develop skills in close reading, literary analysis, making comparative observations, making argumentative thesis statements and communicating thoughts clearly and effectively through writing assignments.
  8. Participate in building a friendly, vibrant and open learning community through collective in-class discussion and other forms of collaborative learning.
  9. Learn about the archival and digital resources at the Penn State library and incorporate the use of these resources in small research projects.
  10. Demonstrate global awareness and intercultural understanding of literature coming from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds to prepare for further study in non-western cultures, comparative literature, and world literature.
  11. Demonstrate a global awareness and intercultural understanding of short stories by establishing connections among literary works form various cultures around the world.
  12. Employ techniques of literary analysis and close reading to develop well-formed argument.
  13. Articulate your own observations, opinions, and assertions in writing and speaking about short stories

General Education objectives

Key literacies
the ability to identify, interpret, create, communicate and compute using materials in a variety of media and contexts. Literacy acquired in multiple areas, such as textual, quantitative, information/technology, health, intercultural, historical, aesthetic, linguistic (world languages), and scientific, enables individuals to achieve their goals, to develop their knowledge and potential, to lead healthy and productive lives, and to participate fully in their community and wider society.
Global learning
the intellectually disciplined abilities to analyze similarities and differences among cultures; evaluate natural, physical, social, cultural, historical, and economic legacies and hierarchies; and engage as community members and leaders who will continue to deal with the intricacies of an ever-changing world. Individuals should acquire the ability to analyze power; identify and critique interdependent global, regional, and local cultures and systems; and evaluate the implications for people’s lives.
Critical and analytical thinking
The habit of mind characterized by comprehensive exploration of issues, ideas, artifacts, and events before accepting or formulating a conclusion. It is the intellectually disciplined process of conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating information gathered from, or generated by, observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication, as a guide to belief and action.
Integrative thinking
The ability to synthesize knowledge across multiple domains, modes of inquiry, historical periods, and perspectives, as well as the ability to identify linkages between existing knowledge and new information. Individuals who engage in integrative thinking are able to transfer knowledge within and beyond their current contexts.
Creative thinking
The capacity to synthesize existing ideas, images, or expertise in original ways and the experience of performing, making, thinking, or acting in an imaginative way that may be characterized by innovation, divergent thinking, and intellectual risk taking.
