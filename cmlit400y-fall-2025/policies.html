Attendance and absences

  • You are allowed up to four (4) absences in this course. For every class missed beyond the allowed four, you will lose 5 percentage points from the “Attendance and Participation” portion of your grade.
  • If you need to miss class for a family emergency, religious holiday, or other personal reason, these will count as part of your four allowed absences. I do not require documentation for absences. Allowance for further absences will be evaluated on a case-by-case basis and may require documentation.
  • If you will be absent from class for scheduled University-approved activities, please notify me ASAP. If you do not notify me in advance, requests to have the absence not count against you or make-up opportunities may be denied. “Notification” means that you must submit a class absence form to me, as per university policy: https://aappm.psu.edu/files/class_absence_v3.pdf
  • Tardiness of more than 5 minutes and early, unexplained departures may count as an absence.
  • Use of your cellphone, email, or other forms of distraction or disruption of the class may count as an absence (unless such use is part of a preauthorized task in class such as when the instructor asks for students to look something up online).
  • Failure to bring the assigned materials to class may count as an absence.
  • Failure to be present in the class without having done the required reading may also count as an absence.

Classroom conduct

This classroom is a place where you will be treated with respect. All members of this class are expected to contribute to a respectful and welcoming environment for every member of the class. Disruptive, aggressive, or otherwise hostile behavior will not be tolerated.

  • Paper copies and printouts of all readings are strongly preferred. Efforts on your behalf have been made to make the materials for this course cheap. Please use the savings to come to class prepared with a functional copy of the materials.
  • If you must use a device (reader, laptop, or multifunction tablet (such as an iPad)), under no circumstances are these to be used for anything other than accessing the readings under discussion or note taking. Repeated violation of this policy will result in your being marked “absent.” It may further result in the revocation of permission to use laptops/tablets for the class as a whole.
  • Even if you only have a digital copy of the reading, you are strongly encouraged to shut off your device and share a paper copy with a neighbor. The purpose of coming to class is to engage directly with the ideas under discussion; screens are often an impediment to this goal.

Late policy

Every day an assignment is late it will be marked down one grade. If the assignment on time would have been an A, it will become an A- after one day, a B+ after two Days, a B after three days and so on until it will become an F on the ninth day.

Communication

You are encouraged to reach out to me in office/zoom hours (listed on the first page of the syllabus) or via email with any questions or issues. I am also available by appointment, so be in touch to arrange a meeting at another time if you cannot come to my office/zoom hours.

I answer most email within 48 hours, but do not regularly check email at night (after 5:00 pm) or on weekends. Do not wait until the last minute to contact me.

Emails asking questions for which the answers may be found in the syllabus or on Canvas WILL NOT BE ANSWERED. So if 48 hours have gone by and you have not received an answer to your question, have a look again at the syllabus and canvas. Chances are you’ll find the answer there.

You are expected to be professional and detailed in your email communication; this includes:

  • Specifying the subject of your email in the heading beginning with “CMLIT 184: …”
  • Use of an appropriate greeting (“Dear Prof. XXX,”; “Hello Professor,”; “Good morning, Prof. XXX,” etc) and sign-off (“Sincerely”; “Thank you” etc)
  • Giving a clear and concise explanation of the purpose of your email, making sure that I have all the information necessary to address your query.

Academic integrity

Students with questions about academic integrity should speak to the instructor and/or visit http://www.la.psu.edu/ and then click on “Academic Integrity.”

Penn State defines academic integrity as “the pursuit of scholarly activity in an open, honest and responsible manner” (Senate Policy 49-20). Dishonesty of any kind will not be tolerated in this course. Dishonesty includes, but is not limited to, cheating, plagiarizing, fabricating information or citations, facilitating acts of academic dishonesty by others, having unauthorized possession of examinations, submitting work of another person or work previously used without permission from the instructor, or tampering with the academic work of other students. Students facing allegations of academic misconduct should not drop the course; those who do will be added to the course again and will be expected to complete course work and meet course deadlines. If the allegations are dismissed, then the drop will be permitted. Students found responsible for academic misconduct often receive academic sanctions, which can be severe, and put themselves at risk for disciplinary sanctions assigned by the University’s Office of Student Conduct (see Senate Policy G-9).

To avoid plagiarism in this course, students should cite any work that is not their own, should never represent the work of others as their own, and always assume that what they turn in will be understood to be their own work.

In this course, you may collaborate with others only when expressly instructed or given permission to do so. When you collaborate, include all collaborators names on submitted work.

AI statement

Generative AI might seem like a shortcut, but shortcuts can mess things up or end up taking longer. In this case what gets cut out is your learning, your growth, and ultimately yourself. The outputs from LLMs can be as inaccurate and biased as the material they were trained on. This tech, which is heavily promoted by big money, relies on stolen property and exploited laborers, and it also has an ever deepening environmental impact (not just on energy but on water too). There’s also increasing evidence that these tools can actually be bad for our brains. All this negative for a tool that is supposed to help make us more efficient as workers. And AI may be able to do that in some sectors even as faulty, spotty, and inefficient as it is today. But job of a being a student is different than many other kinds of work. As a student your job is to learn. Learning can be long and hard, its inefficiencies are what makes it worthwhile. It takes time and dedication to learn something, to truly know it. This journey, the struggle, the dwelling in the unknown for a long time without answers is part of the human experience and this brief moment of your life in college (just four years for most of us) is for many of us the only time in our lives we will have to savor this experience. Using AI risks short cutting yourself on these once in a lifetime experiences.

But on a more practical level, using LLMs can make your work bland, vague, and wrong, as it hinders your skill development. Building skills as a thinker, reader, and writer is what really sets you up for success as a professional, as a citizen, and as a human being. If there is a value to going to college, it is in building such skills. And that development happens through hard work, practice, making mistakes, time, and revision.

Your work in this course should be your own, not that of someone you pay or a bot. Anything that’s not your own will be considered an academic integrity violation and will be evaluated accordingly. And the same goes for all the lectures, assignments, feedback, and grades.

Expectations for classroom behavior

Civil behaviors are expected in the classroom. Do not use computer or other electronic devices for activities unrelated to the class. The Penn State Code of Conduct (http://www.sa.psu.edu/ja/conduct.shtml) prohibits obstruction or disruption of classes (Policy Statement 1). Disruption is defined as “an action or combination of actions by one or more individuals that unreasonably interferes with, hinders, obstructs, or prevents the operation of the University or infringes on the rights of others to freely participate in its programs and services.” Course instructors have the authority to request that a disruptive student leave a class for the remainder of the period. Examples of disruptive behavior include: inappropriate in-class behavior including harassing other students/faculty members, constant interruptions, and the like. Cases of disruptive behaviors will be referred to the Associate Director of Student Affairs for judicial action. Sanctions may include moving the student to another section of the course or administrative removal from the course resulting in a grade of F. Students can direct any questions or concerns they have with the standards of behavior policy to the Associate Director of Judicial Affairs (1-814-863-0342).
