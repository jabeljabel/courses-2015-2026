Course description and objectives

This course considers the infinite variety of stories, poetry, and drama written in and about China, Japan, and Korea through three recurring motifs—fantasy, travel, and war. Our readings, discussions, and presentations will highlight the connections and disjunctions among Asian literatures. In the process, we will consider various questions occasioned by the necessary and formidable project of reading Asian literature. For instance: Where is Asia? When does literature begin? Who can write Asian literature? Why are translations published? What gets translated? Rather than tracing historical trends and similarities of literary traditions over the geography of East Asia, this course deliberately places these literatures into broad categories that transcend time and space. By so doing, the course will reveal the limits and impossibilities of entirely subjective literary readings and suggest the paths to reading literature from other cultures, times, and places. Ultimately, the course will examine what is generally meant by Asian Literature and also the potentials for what Asian Literatures can mean.

This course builds skills in close reading, solid argumentation, and critical thinking about cultural and historical difference. As a medium for enhancing global awareness, the course will also analyze the complexities of interpreting the foreign, tracking cultural influences, and distinguishing traits of various traditions.

Three Modern Masters: Akutagawa Ryunosuke, Lu Xun, and Yi Sang

In the modern period, histories and traditions, experience and trauma, language and geography were so intertwined that it is difficult to speak of a Chinese Literature or a Japanese Literature or a Korean Literature in isolation. And yet in precisely the modern period the notion of a national literature became the major mode of thinking about literature. Why? How do individual authors contradict, subvert, and reinvent their supposed traditions? To what extent is the notion of an Asian Literature justified by what we read? Why are voices overlooked in the notion of a tradition?
