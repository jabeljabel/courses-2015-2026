In class writing

Write your thesis statement. For this class, a thesis is not a statement of fact. It is a declarative statement that interprets facts, opinion, or your paper topic. The thesis is a clear statement of your argument (typically three sentences or less). A thesis must be: a) worth arguing (ie not self-evident to anyone who has read the work of literature); b) provable given the information we have; c) so it is not descriptive but analytical, answering the interpretive questions: “so what?”, “how is this important?”, “why do people need to know this?”; d) supportable within the page limit of this paper.

Due Submit the Thesis, Quotations, and Bibliography before the end of the class session today. (Bonus points possible 2)

In class writing.—Outlining.

Outline your paper. This means write up a one-page document (12 point serif-ed monospace font with one inch margins) that walks your outline reader through what every part of your paper. Generally depending on the logical flow of your argumentation arising from your thesis, the argument (and therefore outline) will have at least three parts and no more than five parts. For a paper of this scale, I expect at least every paragraph to have a separate line in your outline. It might help you to try writing sentences that describe each paragraph in the outline rather than simply bullet points.

Thu, Feb 26

No class

Writing and Research Day.

Write the first draft of your paper. This means walking the reader of your draft through the entire argument in you outline to both show them your ideas and to convince them of your point. Think of this as filling in the argument and making it work. Often arguments change at this stage of writing because we realize that something that seemed ok at the outline stage is unsustainable as you start weaving the argument together with the evidence. Such change is part of the process. But for this class, I want you to note it when it happens in your argumentation.

Due Submit the draft of your Term Paper A. (5 Points)
