Course description

Japanese Film in Time Space

A survey of Japanese film and new media in the twentieth century and beyond, with attention to film history, style, and representation. Taught in English.

This course explores the rich variety of Japanese film and new media. As an introductory survey of Japanese film and new media the course provides a broad historical gloss of developments in the Japanese cinema from early twentieth century silent film through early twenty-first century animated films (animé). Most major Japanese genres (such as contemporary drama, family films, gangster films, school films, and disaster films) will be introduced. Students will become familiar with significant directors, stars, and studios in Japan and also learn the structural components of filmmaking in order to better understand how film makes meaning.

This course thematically examines film and new media both formally (from within) and contextually (from the outside); and further the course breaks down these examinations into examining how film captures time and space and is captured by history and location.

In addition, this course has a significant digital and new media component. Rather than simply reflecting on the products, production, and history of media, we will be using digital methods and tools to aid our analysis. The point of this engagement with the digital is to encourage us to reflect on the differing ways of understanding and insights we might gain through these alternative tools. In other words, our self-reflective use of digital media to understand new media will itself help us understand what such media enable, encourage, disable, and discourage.

Course objectives

Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Identify the roles of media in defining the differences between represented reality and objective reality.
  2. Summarize key concepts related to Japanese film history, film aesthetics, technical aspects of the film medium, and Japanese cultural history.
  3. Explain the differences between space, place, and location and between time, history, and temporality.
  4. Identify media’s role in representation and describe how it differs from other agents of meaning making such as director, studio, star, and audience.
  5. Explain how the environment (location, audience) in which you view a film influences your interpretation of the film
  6. Construct clear arguments in a variety of textual, visual, and digital formats, that reflect an understanding of how different media impact meaning making.
