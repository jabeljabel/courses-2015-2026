Assignments and requirements

Attendance and Participation (20 points)

This is a discussion-based course; attendance and consistent, active participation are required. This means that you will be on time, prepared (reading done and with you in class), and take part in discussion. Simply being present in class is not enough.

16–20
Frequent, substantial, and consistent verbal participation that demonstrates a good grasp of the reading. This includes answering and asking questions as well as taking part in group activities.
11–15
Occasional, inconsistent, or only superficial verbal participation. This includes demonstrating some grasp of the reading but failing to contribute to class discussion and/or group activities.
1–10
Infrequent, inconsistent, or superficial verbal participation. This includes rarely if ever answering or asking questions, failure to grasp the reading or participate in group activities.
0
Very poor or no participation; weak or no grasp of the readings; disruptive, disengaged, or uncooperative behavior.

You are allowed up to four (4) absences in this course. For every class missed beyond the allowed four, you will lose 5 points from the “Attendance and Participation” portion of your grade.

Reading Response Worksheets (10 points)

These are short, written exercises in which you briefly respond to the reading assigned for the assignment the midnight before it is due. You are required to complete ten (10) Reading Response Worksheets during the semester; in other words, students are required to submit responses for about one-third of our roughly 30 sessions. You can choose which readings you’d like to respond to for these assignments. Though the responses may be cited in-class discussion or displayed for other students to see during the session discussing that reading, they will not be graded until the completion of the course giving you the opportunity to rewrite them before assessment. Students are encouraged to work towards submitting all 10 of these responses early in the semester because more time-consuming assignments are due later in the semester. Reading response prompts (worksheets) are available on Canvas and completed forms should be submitted in the appropriate box on Canvas.

Bonus opportunity: Students may submit up to five additional response sheets for a maximum of 5 additional bonus points. These are also due the midnight before an assigned reading is listed on the syllabus.

Creative/AI Writing Assignment (10 points)

This is a class on Japanese Literature. The phrase “Japanese Literature” is both a descriptive and prescriptive mode of understanding literature. It is both a readily understood and highly problematic category. As such, the best way to get at a thorough understanding of this categorization is not only through reading a lot of it, but also through trying our hands at identifying what makes it. We learn differently when we try making things as opposed to simply analyzing them. This creative writing assignment however is not to gauge your abilities as a creative writer, but rather to gauge your ability to reflect on what you learn from the creative process.

For this assignment you have two choices.

  1. Human creative writing. Here you choose a short work or scene from a text on the syllabus and you rewrite it changing something important (modernizing the setting, switching proper names, transforming character genders, changing a plot point from killing to loving or loving to killing etc). Your rewrite should not exceed 500 words. Then write two paragraphs: first explaining why and how you changed the original; second detailing what (if anything) you think was characteristic of “Japanese Literature” about the original and whether or not that element or those elements was/were preserved in your rewrite.
  2. Us a generative language model like ChatGPT to write a short (under 500 word) work of Japanese literature. Specific at least the time period or year, the location, and the plot in your prompt for the AI. Submit your prompt/input, the response/output, and two paragraphs (of your own writing) discussing what characteristics of the output you think the generative language model was considering to be particularly “Japanese”. AI can be useful in showing us what biases, leanings, and stereotypes exist in the present discourse on a subject. In this sense, AI is a great tool for analyzing literary genre. Remembering that generative AI works to produce the most statistically likely sentences around the prompt based on its data set, what do you think the AI’s stereotypes about Japanese literature are? Where do you think they likely come from? Do such stereotypes comport with your readings in this class or clash with them?

Beginning on the first day of class and continuing throughout the semester, we will be collaborating on updating and maintaining a rulebook or list of characteristics we think might be particular to “Japanese Literature” based on our readings and discussions. This will guide us as to how close individual work defines or fulfill the category and how much some works push the boundaries of such preconceived categories. Active participation in creation and maintenance of our rule book is expected. Students who go the extra mile on this project will be eligible for bonus points (up to 5pts for the semester). Remember google doc tracks all of your edits (including deletions and additions). So be a constructive participant in the forum!

Due Thursday, October 31, 2024

Criticism Presentation (10 points)

All students will sign up for one week to introduce new ideas from critical and theoretical sources. On the week for which they’ve signed up and lead critical discussion relating the critical work to the literary work for the week. Students should meet with Professor Abel at least one week prior to their week to confirm the critical piece for presentation is appropriate. These presentations will happen on Thursdays. Students will coordinate with other students who have signed up for that week to present. You may find the bibliography useful for finding a critical piece to discuss. You might also search JSTOR or Project Muse for appropriate articles on the piece of literature you are interested in for that week.

Steps:

  • sign up for a presentation by week three.
  • look at the literature on the schedule for the week you’ve been assigned (Schedule of Readings).
  • choose one piece of literature on the schedule for either the Tuesday or Thursday of that week that you want to read more about.
  • find a scholarly article on the subject of the piece of literature you have chosen. Check the bibliography here, JSTOR, or Project Muse for something appropriate and interesting to you.
  • confirm the critical article is ok with Professor Abel either by coming to his office hours at least one week before or via email.
  • read the work of literature and the critical piece.
  • think about whether or not you think the piece is important, relevant, or useful.
  • organize your thoughts in the form of a presentation.
    • Think of how to best present you the argument of the critical piece you’ve selected.
    • If visual aids might help, you might make a slide show with pictures
    • If an outline of the argument will help, best to create an outline to project for the class
    • If there are keywords or passages from the article, prepare slides with them
  • Your presentation should both:
    • present the argument of the article as best you can, including the important evidence
    • your view of the argument
      • Did you think it was enlightening? Why or why not?
      • Did you agree with it? Why or why not?
      • What evidence did you find convincing?
      • What other literary evidence (not in the critical piece) can you find in the literary text that supports the argument in the article and what other literary evidence in the text do you find that contradicts the argument of the article?

Midterms (30 points)

Check the guidelines.

Mid Term A: Text-based Argumentative Essay (15 points)

  • Choose a text.
  • Choose a theme (such as gender, race, class, history, geography, aesthetics, violence).
  • Find and identify at least three occurrences of your theme in the text.
  • Look for a pattern (a way the text characterizes or deals with the theme) within the three or more occurrences of your theme.
  • Write an argumentative essay suggesting why your theme is important to elucidate meaning from the story. (To generate your argument/thesis, think about what readers might miss if they don’t pay attention to this aspect of the text.) Each of your three or more passages must be woven into your argument/thesis as evidence for your close reading.
  • What context helps you understand and make your argument about this text? Research the context.
  • Be sure to cite/refer to the passages in the text and your context as your evidence.

4-7 pages. MLA Format.

Due Thesis and outline: Thursday, September 12, 2024 (midnight); draft: Thursday, September 26; paper: Tuesday, October 8, 2024

MidTerm B: Contextual Research Essay (15 points)

  • Choose a text.
  • Choose a theme (such as gender, race, class, history, geography, aesthetics, violence).
  • Find and identify at least three occurrences of your theme in the text.
  • Ask yourself what more you need to know in order to understand this theme in the text that is not explained by the details provided within the text alone.
  • Research the details of your theme or your text using library resources and the web.
  • How has what you have read outside the text (the context) changed how you understand the text?
  • Write a convincing argument suggesting why and how your research helps to elucidates the meaning of the story. (To generate your argument/thesis, think about what readers might miss if they don’t pay attention to this context for the text.) Be sure to cite your contextual sources as well as the three occurrences within your text.

4-7 pages. MLA Format.

Due Draft: Thursday, October 24, 2024; paper: Thursday, November 7, 2024

Students will have the opportunity to rewrite their midterms. If the rewrite is an improvement, only the rewrite grade will count towards your final grade. In order to raise your grade, your rewrite must show reaction to the editorial comments on the original. Rewrites are due within two weeks of students receiving their grade and comments on the original.

Final Paper (15 points)

What is Japanese Literature? Based on three or more readings from the class, does the category of “Japanese Literature” mean anything specific to you? You have two options for this paper:

  • Yes, Japanese literature means something to me. If this represents your opinion, then make an argument for what kind of literature Japanese Literature signifies. Elucidate at least three characteristics of Japanese Literature using readings as your evidence.
  • Or.
  • No, Japanese literature is not a compelling category. If this represents your opinion, then frame your argument around what is problematic about the category. Cite at least three readings that seem to violate claims about “Japanese literature” as a category.

In addition to the literature you use as evidence, be sure to react to at least one critical work from our syllabus.

The final paper is final. No rewrites will be allowed for this one.

7-10 pages. MLA Format. Check the guidelines.

Due Thesis and outline: Thursday, November 14, 2024; draft: Thursday, November 21; final paper: December 16, at noon
