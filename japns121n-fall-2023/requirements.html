Individual and group historical timelines

This assignment will help develop your understanding of history and film by getting you to produce basic narratives of Japanese film history. Film histories (like all histories) can be written from a variety viewpoints and source materials. Common film histories include: biographies of stars, directors, producers, and fans — or people’s history; history of labor relations in Japanese film, history of gender in Japanese film—social history; histories of studios, production houses, distribution networks, or film prizes — or institutional history; history of box office returns or film budgets — economic history; histories of styles and content — genre history.

Define the project

For this project, you and your group will determine a branch of Japanese film history in which you have a shared interest.

The topic needs to be broad enough that every member of the group can perform a significant task contributing to the project.

Viable topics include:

  • A history of Nikkatsu studios.
  • A history of the production and reception of one film on our syllabus.
  • A history of Ozu.
  • A history of the Art Theatre Guild (ATG)
  • A history of 1931 in Japanese film.

Weak topics might be:

  • Sessue Hayakawa’s early years
  • A history of Tanizaki and Japanese film

(NOTE: these might be appropriate for one person in a group, but not for an entire group; the group project would have to be pitched a higher level of abstraction such as Film and Authors)

Break down the topic into meaningful tasks for each group member:

Viable group structures might be:

  • For a studio history: Student one does research on the first three decades of the studio, Student two does the second three decades, Student three does the third three decades, Student four does research on a major director from that studio, Student five does research on a major star from the studio, and student six tries to identify overarching trends, themes and motifs that run across the work of the other students. All students then work together to create the timeline.
  • For a history of a major director like Oshima: Students one and two might do research on the biography of the director, students three four and five might do research on the films of the director, students six and seven might work on the technical side finding appropriate images and putting together the spreadsheet and timeline.

When the topic and tasks have been identified, the group or a representative from the group will propose the topic of research to Professor Abel during office hours.

Professor Abel will help the group by identifying good, information rich sources, with accurate information on your research.

Be sure to cite your sources.

Good write ups will explain how your timeline and research enhance our understanding of specific films, directors, and studios in a larger Japanese film history and how it might impact a wider understanding of film.

Post your timeline and write up to the public course website and to canvas.

Module 2: Space In – Framework

This assignment will help you understand the space within films by getting you to examine films closely frame by frame. When people discuss films, they draw on all kinds of evidence to make their points. Sometimes the evidence comes largely from outside of the film, like when the film can only really be understood in the context of other films in a genre, in the historical period in which it was produced, or in an understanding of the politics of the director. Sometimes the evidence for our understanding and explanations of a film come from mainly within the film itself, such as when a particular close up on a gun early in the film is the key to understanding the exciting murder in the climactic scene. Usually people draw on both kinds of evidence (interior and exterior, textual and contextual) to make their points about a film.

This exercise gets you to look squarely at what lies in the frame of a shot from a film in order understand how it is that such a shot might give larger meaning to the whole.

Your group will choose one film from our syllabus.

Then you will first work independently then as a group to finish the module.

Your task for this assignment is twofold. First, you will work individually. Choose a shot from a film we have seen for this class. You will grab the screenshot, annotate the image using film terminology to describe how the frame is composed, and then you will write a convincing explanation in which you use your understanding of the shot and the terminology to justify your reading of the shot, scene, and/or film as a whole.

For bonus points, you may take a photo that repeats the composition of the frame you’ve analyzed. To get points, write a short supplement to your justification of your reading that explains what you have learned about the original film by trying to frame just such a shot yourself.

Second, you will work as a group. You will read the shot analyses of your group mates for different shots from the same film. Then you will work together to try to describe the style of composition for the film. Create a webpage on our site that links to all of the individual shot analysis pages of your group. That hub pages should make an argument about the general style of the film framing and how it relates to the overall meaning of the film. Be sure to cite each individual shot analysis in the summary narrative on this group hub page.

Steps:

  1. Choose a film with your group.
  2. Choose your invididual frame from the film.
  3. Grab the image. (How to do a screenshot on a mac, pc, chromebook)
  4. Decide which film terms from our list are the most useful. (Have a look again at the Yale Film Terms site 1, 2 and 3.)
  5. Mark up the image using your favorite image editor.
    • “Preview” is useful on a mac.
    • Here is a video for chromebook image annotation
    • Windows users might use “microsoft paint”.
    • Everyone could use Canva.
    • there are many many others… use anything that helps you explain what is going on in the frame.
  6. Write your analysis of the image using the terminology (500 words)
  7. Try to answer the following questions in your analysis:
    • How does the composition convey the meaning of the film?
    • What does the composition highlight about the scene, characters, themes, or motifs?
    • Why is this particular way of composing the shot important to the film? Why would other possible camera set-ups not achieve this?
  8. Post the marked up image and your narrative about the image to our website and to canvas.

Group Project:

  • Read individual pages of your groupmates.
  • What have you learned about the film that you would not have understood without the analysis of your peers?
  • What are the patterns identified by more than one of you? The similarities?
  • What are the standalone shots or techniques that seem to stand out? The differences?
  • How do these patterns over the course of the film work to connote the tone of the film?
  • Write a group narrative that explains the style of the film.

Module 3: Temporality or Time in film

We have spent the last few weeks analyzing editing techniques. This assignment will help develop your understanding of time in film by getting you to combine your understanding of shot composition over time and its role in storytelling and narrative.

In film, narration happens through the juxtaposition of successive shots into sequences and sequences into scenes and scenes into the plot. By examining the editing process of one scene and mapping it on a timeline you will be breaking down how a film is composed into some of its core constituent parts. Describing the way films transition between shots and how this relates to the meaning of the film is key to understanding how film works.

Steps:

  1. Identify a scene from a film you and your group would like to work on. Any film from our syllabus is fair game.
  2. Review the editing terms and composition here.
  3. Choose a specific scene for yourself (coordinate with your group so you don’t overlap).
  4. Now make a short storyboard using JS Timeline.
    1. Using the release date of the film (taken ideally from the JMDB, IMDB, or Wikipedia) as the date, assuming noon to be your 0:00 screening start time, mark every new shot with its timestamp in your scene on the timeline google sheet. (you need at least 10 different shot set-ups for your scene or scenes).
    2. Take screenshots of the each new set up. And use the images to illustrate your storyboard.
  5. annotate each image with a brief (less than 50 word explanation of what is going on in terms of editing between the shots)
  6. Generate the storyboard/timeline.
  7. Write a minimum 300 word explanation/narrative of how the editing of your scene furthers the plot and the mood of the film. Be sure to use the film editing terms. Good write ups will explain how specific editing techniques and visual transitions function in terms of the larger plot of the entire film.

Note: Your timeline must have the following to be considered complete:

  • Timeline should be a scene or scene with a minimum of 10 shots.
  • It must be posted to our sites website.
  • The google sheet and link must be shared on canvas.
  • Your minimum 300 word explanation of how the shots work to further the narration.

Your group should then pool the sheets from the timelines/storyboards and make one big storyboard for the chosen film. And write an overarching narrative describing the editing techniques of the film and how the overarching themes and commonalities describe a particular style that functions to enhance the plot. If your chosen film is part of the cinemetrics website data, you might want to compare the shot lengths listed there with your reading.
