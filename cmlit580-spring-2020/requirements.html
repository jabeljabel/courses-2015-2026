In-class participation (50%)

Two content presentations and discussion leading (25%)

Students will sign up for presenting on syllabus readings for two weeks of the semester (12.5% per presentation) (this is in addition to the third presentation for the “Marxism in my field” assignment; see below). Sign ups are to be complete by the second week of class. It is expected that presentations lay out a reading of or argument about at least one of the week’s readings based on their close reading of the material at hand and some outside course material research into relevant context for that reading. Presentations require reading more than everyone else for that week!!! This kind of presentation is a skill that will prepare you for teaching.

Your job is to present your reading/argument to the group and then steer discussion on the text or texts you have taken on. This is your chance to own a section of the class. You can take us in a new direction, continue us down a new line of thinking, or propose a counterargument to something we’ve been discussing. It is up to you. But you must meet with Professor Abel at least one week prior to your presentations to get approval to present. Students who do not fulfill this requirement will not be allowed to present and will fail the assignment. The purpose of the meetings is to vet your ideas first with the Professor so our time in class is not wasted on fruitless discussion or on discussion that is not based on a solid idea and outside research.

An effective presentation develops an interpretation of a text within its historical and/or critical contexts (and therefore will require some outside research as well as close readings of the text at hand). On the day of your presentation, you will also be responsible for monitoring the progress of class discussion, raising questions and helping to address questions under discussion. This is your chance to guide class discussion to your interests, to get your questions answered (and complicated, refined, and developed), and to figure out what you think about the text.

Email the class your presentation outline and/or notes by 5pm on the Wednesday preceding the Thursday class meeting.

The rest of class that is not presenting for a given week is expected to respond actively to the presentation. Responding students are expected to be active and respectful contributors to conversation. This means not only getting your ideas on the table but allowing and encouraging your peers to do the same. Being active and being respectful are not mutually exclusive; it is expected for instance that you allow time for your peers to speak, but it is also expected that you listen carefully to them and respond with insightful (even challenging) questions, constructive comments, and relevant requests for expansion.

Reading notes (25%)

Coming to class prepared (having done the readings, prepared questions, identified key passages of importance/confusion/interest, listed keywords for understanding, identified key themes for the texts) is a necessity for the seminar. A seminar is a communal effort and not a lecture course run by a single professor. Your preparation is assessed through your submitting Reading Notes for 10 weeks of the semester.

Reading Notes are summaries with keywords, quotations, and initial reactions 1–2 pages per week – deposited in the dropbox on canvas by Wednesday at midnight.

To aid your preparation for class discussion (and remembering what you read years from now), reading notes document your reading experience. They can take any form you like, including a full blown reaction essay, simple notes, summaries, a long list of keywords, and/or a list of quotations. But the length shall be no more than two pages per reading.

These notes will be shared with other students for the purpose of making a communal reference guide. As a result, they should not be considered private and may be cited in class discussion.

Written work (50%)

Marxism in my field (10%)

Come to class prepared to answer how the question of marxism impacts my current field of research?

Create an annotated bibliography of articles and books in your field that relate to the subject.

Answer the following questions about these materials: Who is writing them? What are the relevant topics? What is omitted from the topics that form part of the larger topic of marxism? Which of they sources do you need to read for your work? Which can you ignore? Which might you propose we read together as a seminar? How would they fit into our syllabus?

Sign up for a presentation on marxism in my field. Choose a week and add your name to the schedule page on canvas. You can edit it!!! Add one reading of less than 30pages to the date of your presentation.

Project (40%)

Project Option One: term paper

5% Topic statement. This is a foundational part of developing your project. Though not a contract, it will be one of the grounds upon which you build or build away from as you construct your project. The statement should be submitted in the following format or something close to it:

For my seminar paper for CMLIT 570 will examine the problem of Y in theories of marxism through and examination of X. I hope/think that this will develop Y beyond the ideas of Q, R, and S in a way that will account for the nuances of X which the current theories about Y tend to ignore. I also expect that reading X in this way will allow for an approach that has hitherto been ignored.

Define Topic/Scope of Paper

Your seminar paper for this class will take the form an exploration and elaboration of mimetic thinking through one or more cultural products.

Defining the scope of your inquiry early on will provide

You will begin to justify the reason for doing the research here.

5% Thesis statement and outline. This is not the same as your topic statement, though it may derive from it.

10% Close “Working Through” workshop. The point here is to have do a close reading of a passage from a theoretical work from class that you will be working through in your paper. It is not enough to simply do a close reading of the work that shows how you understand the text or argument to be working. Rather you need to go another step. Read with a purpose. How does the text help you and your thesis? How does it work against or contradict your thesis? How does the text need to be changed, rethought, transformed, rewritten to get it to be more in line with your argument? Does this expose a lack in the text or your argument? What are the benefits of transforming the text in this way? (3–5 pages)

Optional: paper draft due Monday after spring break. If you want, I’ll give comments back within two weeks on drafts submitted immediately following spring break.

20% Final draft. Due monday of Finals week. (8,000–12,000 words). MLA or Chicago format.

Project Option Two: final project

40% Project work. To be decided between Option Two-er and Prof. Abel.

In the past I’ve had students do 2 Position Papers for 20%

Participants will post 1000-word arguments during the semester to the canvas website or comparable blog. Students should select readings from the list relevant to their on-going studies. Please post polemics by Wednesday evening prior to the meeting for which they are assigned. A successful polemic presents your entire line of clear argumentation on a topic, following the key turns of your logic. Think of these polemics as addressed to the authors of the text we are reading if it helps you to make a stronger argument. Alternatively, you can think of addressing your classmates to convince them of your opinion. Lastly, you might try direct address to me.

Citations should come primarily from the reading of that week. You might think of these as more formal and lengthy Reading Notes with an explicit argument or as an an undergraduate length critical essay.

And an Extensive Project 20%. To be proposed by Option-Two-er and approved by Professor Abel by the end of the first month of class. The project should showcase knowledge of and engagement with the marxist literary critical or marxist literary tradition as discussed in the course, research into other relevant and related fields or subfields, and innovation and creativity on the theme of marxism. Examples of worthy projects include the production of a fictional work engaging with the ideas we’ve been discussing, a translation and introduction of a key text on marxism that has yet to be translated, or a lengthy interview with a scholar in your field on the topic of marxism.

But this breakdown on evaluation is open to negotiation. For instance, if you are doing a lengthy translation of 50 pages or more and a short introduction, that might be worth the entire 50%.
