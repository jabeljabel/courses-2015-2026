In-class participation (50%)

Participation and presentation and discussion leading (25%)

Students will sign up for presenting on syllabus readings for one week of the semester (this is in addition to the second presentation for the “Ecocriticism and my field” assignment; see below). Sign ups are to be complete by the second week of class. Do so by adding your name under “presenter(s)” on the editable course schedule.

It is expected that presentations lay out a reading of or argument about at least one of the week’s readings based on a close reading of the material at hand in addition to outside course material research into relevant context for that reading. Presentations require reading more than everyone else for that week!!! The “Other Readings” section of the syllabus may be helpful for this outside material; but students may also choose to read the entire book from which a reading was excerpted, reviews of that work, other works by the same writer, or anything else that you deem relevant for helping to make meaning of the readings. This kind of presentation (weaving text and context) is a skill that will prepare you for teaching.

Your job is to present your reading/argument to the group and then steer discussion on the text or texts you have taken on. This is your chance to “own” a section of the class. You can take us in a new direction, continue us down a new line of thinking, or propose a counterargument to something we’ve been discussing. It is up to you.

But you must meet with Professor Abel at least one week prior to your presentations to get approval to present. Students who do not fulfill this requirement will not be allowed to present during their week and will fail the assignment. The purpose of the meetings is to vet your ideas first with the Professor so our time in class is not wasted on fruitless discussion or on discussion that is not based on a solid reading and outside research. You are not expected to have a full presentation prepared for this meeting, but you should have begun to read and formulate an outline of the presentation.

Email the class your presentation outline and/or notes by 5pm on the Wednesday preceding the Thursday class meeting.

On the day of your presentation, you will also be responsible for monitoring the progress of class discussion, raising questions and helping to address questions under discussion. This is your chance to guide class discussion to your interests, to get your questions answered (and complicated, refined, and developed), and to figure out what you think about the text.

The rest of class that is not presenting for a given week is expected to respond actively to the presentation. Responding students are expected to be engaged and respectful contributors to conversation. This means not only getting your ideas on the table, but also allowing and encouraging your peers to do the same. Being active and being respectful are not mutually exclusive; it is expected, for instance, that you allow time for your peers to speak, but it is also expected that you listen carefully to them and respond with insightful (even challenging) questions, constructive comments, and relevant requests for expansion and citation of the evidence.

Reading notes (25%)

Coming to class prepared (having done the readings, prepared questions, identified key passages of importance/confusion/interest, listed keywords for understanding, identified key themes for the texts) is a necessity for the seminar.

A seminar is a communal effort and not a lecture course run by a single professor. The sum of our communal commitment to the topics and readings is a measure of the success of the course. To this end our preparation is essential. Your preparation is assessed through your submitting “Reading Notes” for 5 weeks of the semester (5 points each).

Reading Notes are summaries with keywords, quotations, and initial reactions 1–2 pages per week – posted on canvas discussion board by the Wednesday at midnight before the assigned reading is due.

To aid your preparation for class discussion (and remembering what you read years from now), reading notes document your reading experience. They can take any form you like, including a full blown reaction essay, simple notes, summaries, a long list of keywords, and/or a list of quotations. But the length per week shall be no more than two pages per reading.

These notes are shared with the class on the discussion board for the purpose of making a communal reference guide. As a result, they should not be considered private and may be cited by other students or the professor in class discussion.

Written work (50%)

Ecocriticism and my field (10%)

Sign up for a presentation on “ecocriticism in my field.” Choose a week for a presentation of this project and add your name to the schedule page on canvas. You can edit it!!!

Create an annotated bibliography of articles and books in your field that relate to the subject. No more than 50 references. Organize it into categories or subfields. Write headnotes for the categories that describe the kind of work for which you are creating a list. (Use a bibliographic software for this such as Zotero or Endnote to organize. Share the reference file in .ris format with the class).

Answer the following questions about these materials: Who is writing them? What are the relevant topics? What is omitted from the topics that form part of the larger topic of ecocriticism? Which of the sources do you need to read for your work? Which can you ignore? Which might you propose we read together as a seminar? How would they fit into our syllabus?

Come to class prepared to answer: how the question of ecocriticism impacts my current field of research and my course project?

Due Submit your finished annotated bibliography on canvas by Nov. 18.

Project (40%)

There are two options for a project for this course.

Project Option One: term paper

5% Topic statement. This is a foundational part of developing your paper. Though not a contract, it will be one of the grounds upon which you build or build away from as you construct your argument. The statement should be submitted in the following format or something close to it:

For my seminar paper, I will examine the problem of Y in theories of ecocriticism through and examination of X. I hope/think that this will develop Y beyond the ideas of Q, R, and S in a way that will account for the nuances of X which the current theories about Y tend to ignore. I also expect that reading X in this way will allow for an approach that has hitherto been ignored.

Define Topic/Scope of Paper

Your seminar paper for this class will take the form an exploration and elaboration of ecocritical thinking through one or more cultural products.

Every paper cannot be about everything. Limits are necessary to build a convincing project. Defining the scope of your inquiry early on will provide you the ground from which to build. You will begin to justify the reason for doing the research here.

5% Introduction with thesis statement and outline. This is not the same as your topic statement, though it may derive from it. It should be a refinement or further elaboration of the ideas you struggled with in the topic statement. It can give more context and significance as well as provide your reader a sense of where the paper is going to go, what kind of evidence it will cite, and a sense of the scope of the conclusions likely to arise.

10% Close “Working Through” workshop. The point here is to have do a close reading of a passage from a theoretical work from class that you will be working through in your paper. It is not enough to simply do a close reading of the work that shows how you understand the text or argument to be working.

Rather you need to go another step. Read with a purpose. How does the critical text help you and your thesis? How does it work against or contradict your thesis? How does the text need to be changed, rethought, transformed, rewritten to get it to be more in line with your argument? Does this expose a lack in the text or your argument? What are the benefits of transforming the text in this way? (3–5 pages)

Optional: paper draft due Monday after Thanksgiving break. If you want, I’ll give comments back within two weeks on drafts submitted immediately following break. This is for your benefit!

20% Final draft. Due Monday of Finals week. (8,000–12,000 words). MLA or Chicago format.

Project Option Two: final project

This is a more flexible option for students who are writing too many papers, want to try something new, are currently studying for comps, or are dissertating on a topic unrelated to ecocriticism.

2 Position Papers for 20% (10% each). Participants will post 1000-word arguments during the semester to the canvas website or comparable blog. Students should select readings from the list relevant to their on-going studies. Please post polemics by Wednesday evening prior to the meeting for which they are assigned. A successful polemic presents your entire line of clear argumentation on a topic, following the key turns of your logic. Think of these polemics as addressed to the authors of the text we are reading if it helps you to make a stronger argument. Alternatively, you can think of addressing your classmates to convince them of your opinion. Lastly, you might try direct address to me.

Citations should come primarily from the reading of that week. You might think of these as more formal and lengthy Reading Notes with an explicit argument or as an undergraduate length critical essay.

And an Extensive Project 20%. To be proposed by Option-Two-er and approved by Professor Abel by the end of the first month of class. The project should showcase knowledge of and engagement with the eco critical or eco literary tradition as discussed in the course, research into other relevant and related fields or subfields, and innovation and creativity on the theme of ecocriticism. Examples of worthy projects include the production of a fictional work engaging with the ideas we’ve been discussing, a translation and introduction of a key text on ecocriticism that has yet to be translated, or a lengthy interview with a scholar in your field on the topic of ecocriticism.

But this breakdown on evaluation is open to negotiation. For instance, if you are doing a lengthy translation of 50 pages or more and a short introduction, that might be worth the entire 40%.
